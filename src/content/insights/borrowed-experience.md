There are two ways to learn how work actually works. You can make every mistake yourself, one painful quarter at a time. Or you can borrow the experience of people who already made them.

The first way is how most careers go. Not because people are stubborn, but because nobody told them the second way existed.

Here is how borrowed experience works in practice.

**Find the person two steps ahead, not twenty.** The senior vice president's advice is interesting and mostly useless to you; the job market and the company she came up in are gone. The person who got promoted eighteen months ago, from your seat, in your company, knows exactly which doors are real and which are painted on the wall. Buy that person coffee.

**Ask about the mistake, not the success.** "How did you get promoted?" gets you a modest shrug. "What do you wish you had done differently your first year here?" gets you twenty minutes of gold, because everyone has that list and nobody ever asks for it.

**Ask what it looked like from the other side.** When your manager explains a decision, ask what they were weighing. Not to argue. To learn what the view is like from a chair you have not sat in yet. Most early-career confusion is not about skill. It is about not knowing how your work looks from across the desk.

**Write it down and reread it in six months.** Advice you cannot use yet is still advice. The note you took in February about office politics will make perfect, painful sense in August.

**Pass it down.** The moment someone is two steps behind you, you have experience worth borrowing. Offer it. It is how the whole system keeps working, and it is the fastest way to find out how much you have actually learned.

Joe spent years mentoring new hires at Cisco: the smartest kids in the building, walking into a Fortune 100 with no idea how it worked. Every one of them who did well had figured out how to borrow. The ones who struggled were trying to earn every lesson the hard way.

You do not have to. That is the whole point of the book.
