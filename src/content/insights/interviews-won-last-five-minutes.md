"So, do you have any questions for us?"

Most candidates hear that line as the end of the interview. They relax, ask about vacation days or say "No, I think you covered everything," and head for the door. In doing so they skip the one part of the interview they actually control.

The last five minutes are where you get to decide what they remember. Use them.

**Bring three real questions, written down.** Not questions you could answer from the company website. Questions about the work itself. "What does a great first six months look like in this role?" "What is the hardest part of this job that is not in the posting?" "What separates people who do well on this team from people who struggle?" Bring them on paper. It is fine to look at it.

**Listen to the answer and respond to it.** A question you ask and then ignore is just a box you checked. Follow up: "That makes sense. In my internship I ran into something similar, and here is what I did." Now the question has turned into proof that you fit.

**Repair a weak answer.** If something earlier went badly, this is your chance. "I want to go back to the question about deadlines. I gave you a vague answer, and I have a better example." It shows self-awareness and composure, the exact two things a nervous answer made them doubt.

**Say plainly that you want the job.** Do not assume they know. "I have heard enough today to know I want this role. Is there anything that would hold you back from moving me forward?" It feels bold. It is also the only question that tells you which concern you need to answer before you leave the room.

**Ask about next steps and timing.** "What happens from here, and when should I expect to hear back?" Now you know when to follow up, and you will not spend two weeks refreshing your inbox and guessing.

Then send a short thank-you note the same day. Mention one specific thing from the conversation so it is clearly not a template.

The candidates who get offers are not always the most qualified people in the building that day. Often they are the ones who finished strong. Interviewers are human, and the last thing they heard is usually the first thing they recall when they sit down to compare notes.

So prepare your ending as carefully as your opening. It might count for more.
