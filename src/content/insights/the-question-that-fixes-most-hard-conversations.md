People prepare for hard conversations by rehearsing lines. The opener, the evidence, the comeback for the comeback. Then the conversation goes somewhere unscripted in the first forty seconds and the whole script burns down.

Skip the script. Answer one question instead: **what do I want them to do when this conversation is over?**

Not "what do I want to say." Not "what do I want them to understand." What do I want them to do.

Watch what that one question does to the usual disasters.

**The venting session in disguise.** If the honest answer is "I just want them to know how frustrating this is," you do not have a conversation yet. You have a feeling. Legitimate, but a feeling with no requested action lands as an accusation, and accused people defend. Find the action first, even a small one, and the same frustration becomes workable.

**The ambush.** If the answer is "admit they were wrong," put the car keys down. Nobody does that, including you. Convert it: what would they do differently next time, specifically? Chase the behavior, not the confession, and suddenly there is somewhere for the other person to go besides the bunker.

**The mush.** Most hard conversations fail soft, not loud. Thirty minutes of carefully cushioned hints, both people nodding, nothing changing, because no one ever said the thing. When you know the ask, you can do what cushioning never does: say it once, plainly, early. "I need the Friday reports to be right the first time. Let's figure out what is getting in the way." One sentence of clarity buys you twenty-nine minutes of actual problem-solving.

The ask also sets your temperature. People escalate in hard conversations because they are navigating without instruments. Volume substitutes for direction. When you know exactly where the conversation is supposed to land, you can afford to be calm in the turbulence. You know the airport.

So, before the next one: one sticky note. "When this is over, I want them to ______." If you cannot fill the blank, you are not ready, and no amount of rehearsed dialogue will fix that. If you can fill it, you barely need the rehearsal.

Say the thing. Make the ask. Solve it together. That is the whole technology.
